Targeted Bus-Cycle Interception: A Low-Latency WebAssembly Bridge for 16-Bit Bare-Metal Audio Synthesis

Akash Kumar, Aryan Jagtap, Atharva Gaikwad, Shravani Phadtare, Shweta Patil
Department of Multidisciplinary Engineering / AI & DS
Vishwakarma Institute of Technology (Autonomous Institute Affiliated to SPPU), Pune, India
Project Faculty Guide: Prof. Gopal Upadhye
Abstract—
The 16-bit 8086 microprocessor remains a fundamental cornerstone in computer systems pedagogy, embedded systems, and deterministic cycle-accurate machine organization. However, legacy x86 audio execution is historically constrained by two divergent extremes: physical bare-metal execution is restricted to harsh monophonic 1-bit square waves, whereas existing browser-based emulators (such as v86 and DOSBox-Wasm) impose a severe “Virtualization Tax” requiring >140 MB of memory and introducing 60–120 ms of buffer dispatch jitter. In this paper, we present Crimson Orbit, a dual-state computing platform that bridges bare-metal real-mode 8086 assembly with modern WebAudio DSP. Rather than simulating an entire motherboard, our approach introduces Targeted Bus-Cycle Interception: a lightweight WebAssembly execution bridge trapping I/O port writes to the Intel 8253 PIT (Port 42h) and Intel 8255 PPI (Port 61h), serializing them into an ultra-compact 4-byte micro-packet (⟨CMD, DIV_LO, DIV_HI, DURATION⟩). We implement an interactive 60-note in-RAM circular tape sequencer (composer.asm) running bare-metal without OS interrupts, alongside a dual-engine synthesis architecture supporting cycle-accurate 1-bit Galois LFSR square waves and 44.1 kHz acoustic resynthesis (Steinway Grand Piano, Martin Guitar, Ludwig Drums). Empirical evaluation across 1,000 runs confirms that Crimson Orbit achieves a mean bus-trapping latency of 0.889 μs, an end-to-end dispatch latency of 11.8 ms (a 6.3× improvement over full VMs), and a memory footprint of just 12.4 MB (an 82% to 91% reduction).
Index Terms—WebAssembly, 8086 Assembly, Bus-Cycle Interception, Low-Latency Audio, Web Audio API, Microprocessor Virtualization, Acoustic Resynthesis, Embedded Systems Pedagogy.

I. Introduction

UNDERGRADUATE computer engineering curricula mandate hands-on experiential mastery of low-level machine organization, register manipulation, and peripheral bus input/output (I/O) interfacing, as formalized in the IEEE/ACM CS2023 guidelines [1]. For over four decades, the Intel 8086 real-mode architecture has remained the premier pedagogical target due to its direct hardware transparency and lack of preemptive operating system abstractions.

However, legacy audio synthesis in bare-metal x86 environments faces a fundamental physical constraint: the original IBM PC audio subsystem couples the Intel 8253 Programmable Interval Timer (PIT) Channel 2 and the Intel 8255 Programmable Peripheral Interface (PPI) directly to a 1-bit electromagnetic cone [2]. Consequently, software executing on bare-metal hardware can only modulate binary square-wave pulses (+5V and 0V), yielding an abrasive, monophonic acoustic output colloquially referred to as “chiptune beeps.”

To make vintage software accessible without physical 1980s hardware, modern educational systems rely on browser-based virtualization platforms, notably v86 [3] and DOSBox-Wasm [4]. However, these monolithic emulators suffer from the “Virtualization Tax”: they allocate large contiguous memory buffers (64–128 MB) to simulate floppy controllers, IDE drives, VGA video cards, and protected-mode paging. Because audio packet generation is locked to the 60 Hz display quantum, these systems introduce 60 to 120 ms of dispatch jitter, rendering real-time musical performance or auditory feedback impossible [5].

This paper presents Crimson Orbit, an architectural framework that resolves this 40-year dilemma through three technical contributions:

1) Targeted Bus-Cycle Interception: An ultra-lightweight execution bridge that isolates the 8086 execution loop down to audio I/O ports 42h and 61h, bypassing motherboard emulation and packaging bus cycles into a 4-byte micro-packet.
2) Acoustic Transduction Engine: A real-time resynthesis pipeline translating discrete countdown divisors into 44.1 kHz multi-timbral PCM instruments with sub-12ms latency.
3) In-RAM Composition without OS Services: An interactive 60-note circular tape sequencer (composer.asm) executing purely in 16-bit real-mode RAM without DOS INT 21h interrupts.

II. Related Work & Gaps

A. Client-Side x86 Virtualization: Hemmer [3] introduced client-side PC virtualization via WebAssembly in v86. While achieving impressive instruction coverage, v86 incurs an idle memory footprint exceeding 140 MB and high audio latency due to monolithic hardware emulation. Yan et al. [6] analyzed Wasm performance across complex workloads, confirming that cross-boundary serialization incurs microsecond overheads if payloads exceed several kilobytes.

B. Low-Latency Web Audio: Buffa, Kleimola et al. [5], [7] demonstrated that dedicated AudioWorklet threads operating on 128-sample quanta can achieve sub-15ms latency in browser environments. However, existing Web Audio Modules (WAMs) target modern MIDI controllers, leaving legacy hardware timer cycles unaddressed.

C. Chiptune & Sound Reconstruction: Donahue et al. [8] developed the NES-MDB corpus by extracting register writes from vintage 6502 assembly code and mapping them to multi-instrumental MIDI files. However, their transcription was an offline analysis pipeline, not an interactive, real-time live execution system.

D. Research Gaps: Existing literature exhibits four critical gaps: G1 (Virtualization Tax) in monolithic emulators; G2 (1-Bit Timbral Ceiling) on bare-metal PC speakers; G3 (Pedagogical Disconnect) in register-only simulators like EMU8086 [9]; and G4 (Static Playback) in legacy assembly music lacking in-RAM composition.

III. System Architecture

A. Bare-Metal 8086 Control-Plane

The core assembly subsystem is autonomous and OS-independent. A 512-byte MBR bootloader (Source/boot.asm) is loaded by system BIOS at physical address 0000:7C00h. It switches drive heads and reads 30 sequential kernel sectors (15,031 bytes) into execution segment 1000:0000h.

Tone generation operates by configuring the Intel 8253 PIT Mode Register (Port 43h) with control word 0B6h (Channel 2, Mode 3 square wave, LSB then MSB). The timer decrements from a master clock $f_{\text{osc}} = 1,193,180\text{ Hz}$:

Divisor = \text{round}\left(\frac{1,193,180}{f_{\text{desired}}}\right)

The divisor low byte and high byte are written to Port 42h, and speaker output is gated by asserting bits 0 and 1 on PPI Port 61h.

; Intel 8253/8255 Tone Driver (Source/speaker.asm) mov dx, 0012h ; High word of 1,193,180 mov ax, 34DCh ; Low word of 1,193,180 div bx ; AX = Divisor, BX = Freq in Hz mov al, 0B6h out 43h, al ; PIT Mode 3 Square Wave mov al, bl out 42h, al ; Divisor LSB mov al, bh out 42h, al ; Divisor MSB in al, 61h or al, 03h ; Gate On (Bits 0 & 1) out 61h, al

B. Targeted 4-Byte Micro-Packet Protocol

Whenever the CPU encounters opcode 0xE6 0x42 or 0xE6 0x61, the execution bridge intercepts the bus cycle. Rather than generating an analog pulse, it serializes the transaction into an immutable 4-byte micro-packet:

\text{Packet} = \langle \text{CMD}, \text{DIV}_{\text{LO}}, \text{DIV}_{\text{HI}}, \text{DURATION} \rangle

Byte 0 encodes command state (0x01 = Tone On, 0x00 = Tone Off, 0x02 = Galois Noise Burst); Bytes 1 and 2 store divisor LSB and MSB; Byte 3 encodes quantized step duration in 10ms units.

C. In-RAM Tape Sequencer (composer.asm)

To resolve Gap G4, we implement an interactive 60-note circular tape sequencer in raw 8086 RAM. The memory array Song_CustomUser stores discrete word pairs [Freq, Dur] terminated by 0FFFFh, 0. The Web Studio features a bidirectional binary deserializer mapping this RAM buffer into an 8-track, 16-step matrix sequencer.

IV. Dual-Engine Audio Transduction

A critical challenge in peer review is proving that sound originates from low-level assembly logic rather than high-level web samples. Crimson Orbit solves this through a bifurcated dual-engine architecture:

1) Authentic 1-Bit Mode (Zero Samples): Generates pure cycle-accurate mathematical square waves directly from the intercepted divisor ($f = 1,193,180 / \text{Divisor}$). Percussion sounds are generated via a JavaScript port of the 16-bit Galois Linear Feedback Shift Register (LFSR seed 0ACE1h) matching Source/speaker.asm, synthesizing snare wires and cymbals purely through pseudo-random digital noise.

2) Acoustic Resynthesis Mode: Maps discrete countdown frequencies to a multi-timbral WebAudio graph featuring 44.1 kHz PCM acoustic models: a Steinway Grand Piano, Martin Acoustic Guitar, and Ludwig Studio Drum Kit with real-time FFT spectrum visualization.

V. Empirical Evaluation

The system was evaluated using an automated profiling harness (Tools/benchmark_metrics.py) across 1,000 consecutive bus-trapping iterations using high-precision nanosecond timers.

As documented in Table I, Crimson Orbit slashes the memory footprint from 142.6 MB (v86) and 68.2 MB (DOSBox) down to 12.4 MB—a reduction of 82% to 91%. This drastic efficiency stems directly from eliminating motherboard chip emulation (VGA, DMA, IDE).

End-to-end audio dispatch latency dropped to 11.8 ms, comprising 0.889 μs micro-packet serialization and a 10.9 ms AudioWorklet quantum. Because this latency falls well below the 15–20 ms human psychoacoustic threshold, Crimson Orbit enables authentic real-time musical performance without the sluggishness of traditional virtual machines.

VI. Conclusion & Future Work

Crimson Orbit demonstrates that bare-metal 16-bit 8086 assembly computing can be liberated from both physical 1-bit buzzer constraints and the bloat of monolithic virtual machines. By coupling targeted bus-cycle interception with a 4-byte micro-packet protocol, our architecture achieves an 11.8 ms dispatch latency, an 82%–91% reduction in memory overhead, and an in-RAM circular tape composition environment. Future work will extend the micro-interception specification to emulated Sound Blaster OPL3 frequency modulation registers and multi-client WebRTC jam sessions.
